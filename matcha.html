Every Recession Needs a Small Treat Superstar

First, it was fro-yo. Now it's matcha. Is every economic downturn accompanied by a collectively agreed-upon little treat?

BY KAYA 5 MIN READ

The worldwide matcha industry is projected to reach $7.43 billion by 2030. Scroll through Instagram or TikTok and you'll find no shortage of pastel-green drinks being photographed, reviewed, ranked, and enthusiastically consumed. There are matcha maps, matcha rankings, matcha pilgrimages. People are opening matcha cafes, developing matcha menus, and building entire identities around a finely whisked bowl of powdered green tea.

I couldn't help but wonder: Is matcha the new fro-yo?

I've been thinking about how, every decade or so, we seem to collectively appoint one small treat to get us through the economic circumstances of the time.

During the last recession, it was frozen yogurt. Suddenly, fro-yo shops were everywhere.( I even had the pleasure of working at one in Houston.) We had self-serve machines, endless toppings, colorful interiors, and the freedom to construct a dessert that was somehow both a personal expression and a $5 purchase. It was a little luxury that felt accessible. You weren't buying a new wardrobe or booking a vacation. You were buying a cup of frozen yogurt with an unreasonable number of toppings.

And now, perhaps, it's matcha.

There's something particularly interesting about the economics of a small treat. When larger purchases become harder to justify, smaller pleasures can become more appealing. The apartment is still expensive. The flight is still out of reach. The general state of the world remains questionable. But a $5 matcha? That's manageable.

It's the affordable luxury. A little ritual. Something to look forward to.

But I think there's another part of this phenomenon worth examining: we don't simply enjoy these things. We take them all the way.

A small treat becomes an industry. An industry becomes an aesthetic. An aesthetic becomes a business opportunity. Suddenly, everyone wants a piece of it.

People aren't just drinking matcha; they're opening matcha cafes, importing ceremonial-grade powders, designing minimalist green interiors, and building businesses around the idea that we might all want to drink this particular beverage every morning for the foreseeable future.

Some of these businesses will inevitably close. Some will never make it past the trend cycle. Does that mean they shouldn't have existed in the first place?

I don't think so.

Not every business needs to become a permanent institution. Not every cultural moment needs to produce a hundred-year-old company. Sometimes a thing exists because, for a particular moment in time, a lot of people wanted it. And sometimes that's enough.

There's something quite human about collectively deciding that we're going to make a big deal out of something small. Especially when so many other things feel outside our control.

Maybe the small treat isn't really about the treat at all. Maybe it's about having something to anticipate, something to participate in, something that makes an otherwise ordinary Tuesday feel a little more interesting.

Which brings me to a question I've been entertaining:

Does every recession have a small treat superstar?

And if so, who's next?

Because eventually, we'll move on from matcha. The cafes will change hands, the menus will get redesigned, and the internet will find another beverage to photograph from above.

But I'm willing to bet we'll find something else to obsess over.

Until then, I'll be keeping an eye for the next small delight.
